Why Are Babysitters So Rare in India?

Parenting 27 Oct 2026 · 7 min read · Team Pacifyco

Urban India has put almost everything on call: a cab at the tap of a screen, house help on a monthly plan, a cook by the week, an electrician by evening and a salon in the living room. So how did we forget to invent the everyday babysitter?

We have encountered this fact more times than we'd like to, but there is art in repetition, and repetition is the only way one can attempt to create change. The Indian mother is, by any honest accounting, the most overworked and under-thanked professional in the country. She often works a second full-time shift that nobody pays for, and that nobody thinks of relieving her of.

There is barely a concept of giving her three or four hours a day to herself without making it seem like a luxury. This piece is an argument for doing exactly that, and for asking why the humble babysitter, so ordinary in Sydney or Seattle, still feels like a slightly scandalous idea in Surat or Secunderabad.

How the West Hired the Girl Next Door

Early records describe two high-school girls in an American neighbourhood who hired themselves out for twenty-five cents an evening so that couples could go out for the evening. The verb "to babysit" came much later. So the noun was born before the act had a name, which makes it safe to assume that the need for her had always been there, and the language simply took time to catch up.

What made her? Three things, mostly.

  • In the 1920s and '30s, more American children stayed in high school longer, and a distinct youth culture emerged with its own money, music and wants. A girl of eighteen now had both free evenings and a reason to earn.
  • After the Second World War, the baby boom filled homes with toddlers, often miles away from their grandparents. These families actually ran short of teenage sitters because there were far more babies than teens available.
  • Postwar couples began to treat a few hours alone together as a normal and healthy part of married life, giving rise to a culture of couples spending time and money on Friday-night films and dinner parties.

The West then did what it does best: it turned a habit into an institution. The American Red Cross still runs a Babysitter's Training course for teens, and Australia has a Working With Children Check. India may have fallen slightly behind.

Why did it stick so well? Because it solved two problems at once: parents got trusted, affordable, short-notice help, and young people earned money to meet rising living costs.

The Western model also brought training courses, references, word of mouth and clear house rules, which let ordinary parents say yes with confidence. In London or Melbourne, there are apps where parents can find a babysitter nearby in minutes. This is exactly what Pacifyco is trying to build in India.

Our Society Always Had Sitters. We Just Never Called Them That.

Our ancestors lived in joint families, with a grandmother as principal, two aunts as faculty and a rotating cast of cousins available for childcare. Many of us have been babysitters ourselves, or have had an elder sister or cousin look after us while the adults cooked. And in every railway colony and housing society, there was a neighbour's door you could knock on: "Just watch him for an hour, I'm going to the market."

Notice the pattern: help always came from someone familiar, and the children's favourite was usually the youngest of them, the elder sister or cousin. Trained babysitters are, in a sense, that cousin who came for the holidays, made reliable, vetted and available on a regular basis.

What changed in the Indian family

The joint family worked because of arithmetic, but three significant shifts quietly broke that arithmetic. First, couples moved to cities for work, often far away from their parents. Second, both partners now work, so there is no one left at home by default. Third, families got smaller, so there are fewer elder siblings and cousins to share the childcare load.

I am not claiming grandparents have disappeared. They still fly in every now and then, but they are older and have their own health and lives to look after. So, slowly, the older form of help is fading, and nothing is replacing it.

Babysitters Are Affordable and Flexible. So Why Don't We Have Them?

On paper, a babysitting service may be the most sensible childcare idea India has not yet adopted. You pay only for the hours you actually use, much like the gig services we have already grown used to. You can book help for the afternoons after preschool, the evenings when work runs late, or the weeks when the grandparents have gone home. And since the sitter is a student earning pocket money, the arrangement is lighter on the household in every sense.

There are five reasons this hasn't taken off yet, in ascending order of stubbornness.

  1. No infrastructure. The West built background checks, Working With Children cards and Red Cross certificates, but India has nothing comparable for a casual sitter. Even in Bangalore, parents looking for trusted babysitter services mostly find nanny services built for full-time, live-in work, a different need altogether.
  2. Families have changed. For decades, middle-class Indian homes relied on family members or a full-time helper. Now, with young parents living independently and families getting smaller, the demand for flexible help is going up.
  3. Part-time work isn't the norm for students. In Sydney, an 18-year-old serving coffee or minding toddlers is building character. In India, the same young person is often discouraged, because the work is seen as domestic labour. This is changing with today's teenagers.
  4. Mom guilt. Paying someone so you can rest and relax, or have dinner with your husband, still feels to many like an unnecessary luxury that needs a reason.
  5. The village hangover. We assume help should come free, from family, as a matter of duty. Paying for it can feel like admitting the family has failed. However, many new mothers are moving away from this way of thinking.

The Babysitter as an Older Sister

Let us consciously move away from the image of the babysitter as a stern stranger with a clipboard. The right sitter is someone India already has a word for: didi, chechi, akka, the older sister. She is fun, warm and trustworthy. She is not there to discipline, nor to replace you, but to be the cool cousin who arrives with a sketchbook and leaves with a fan club.

A sitter who is nineteen, twenty or a little older sits in a sweet spot the child rarely meets: someone who is grown up but not a parent, who still remembers being small, and who speaks the child's language of games, songs and secrets.

And a good sitter brings:

  • Help with homework. Maths explained by someone who did it three years ago, minus the parental sigh.
  • A love of reading. A sitter who reads aloud every day, does the voices and lets the child turn the pages builds the one habit all parents love.
  • Healthy routines. Meals on time and a nap when it's due mean parents come home to a child who has eaten properly, not one running on biscuits.
  • Sensible screen habits. Creative play, art, clay and building forts from cushions, with the family's screen rules actually followed.
  • Manners and sharing. Please, thank you, waiting your turn, greeting elders and sharing toys are habits children copy from the people they admire.
  • Handling big feelings. Naming emotions, calming down after a tantrum, losing a game gracefully, all modelled with patience.
  • Getting moving. Outdoor play, a walk in the park, cycling, running, dancing. A young sitter has the energy a tired parent often runs out of.
  • Values, too. A sitter who shares the family's culture can tell the Panchatantra story, help with the Diwali rangoli or sing the same lullaby your mother sang. Values are reinforced by repetition, and a didi is one more voice repeating them.

And for parents, the quietest benefit of all: a short, honest update at the end of each day on how the child ate, slept, played and behaved.

A Mother Is Not a Monument

The babysitter came late to India not because Indian parents love their children more, or because they trust the world less, but because for a long time we had the joint family, the cousins and the neighbour aunty. Those arrangements are fading, and they are creating a new need.

So let us make three small, sensible changes. Let us build the trust systems the West took for granted: verified sitters on a childcare platform that lets parents hire a babysitter as easily as they book a cab. Let us treat a college student who minds children for pocket money not as someone doing "servant's work", but as a young person learning responsibility.

And let us glorify the idea of a well-rested mother. A rested mother is not a lesser mother but a more patient, more present and more joyful one.
